📋 Sermon Overview

Central Theme: The last week of Selah sits with Psalm 34 in the Cave of Adullam — where David’s distressed, indebted, bitter-souled men first learn the fear of the Lord. Reverence is not an idea in the head. It is cultivated: restrain and redirect the tongue, do justice, and pursue shalom until dependence on God grows and fear of circumstances shrinks.

Baptism Sunday, last Selah: The service included baptisms before the word. Kevin’s charge is the series’ close: pause, praise, and learn the fear of the Lord from the middle of the psalm — then live it until God looks larger than the horde outside the cave.

🌟 Main Takeaways

  1. Selah is a hymnal pause. Psalms (Tehillim, praises) were Israel’s public songs. Selah can mean rest — or the moment the assembly joins in.
  2. Psalm 34 belongs in a cave. The superscription places it after David’s feigned madness before Abimelek. The “mighty men” began as outcasts around a rejected leader.
  3. Fear of the Lord is reverence — dread and fascination together, not mere fright, and not an abstraction that never reaches the mouth, the oppressed, or ordinary work.
  4. Three practices cultivate it: restrain and redirect the tongue toward specific praise; do good as justice; seek shalom — things as they were designed to be.
  5. The last verse breaks the acrostic on purpose: God redeems those who take refuge in him. Fearing God shrinks self-reliance and grows daily dependence on the cross.

⏸️ Selah: A Hymnal Meant for Public Praise

Kevin opened the last sermon in Selah with the whole of Psalm 34. The book of Psalms is a collection of poems and songs. The Hebrew name is Tehillim — praises. Before PowerPoint, churches held a Bible and a hymnal in the pew. He pictured Israel’s worship that way: an oral culture, songs memorized, trained singers carrying the full piece, the congregation joining the refrain.

Some scholars hear selah as rest. Others hear it as the cue for the whole assembly to come in. Either way, this hymnal exists for the public praise of God. If someone is opening the Bible for the first time, Kevin would still start them in a Gospel — but Psalms is a close second: 150 poems from a poet trying to capture human experience in language that stays with you when you are in difficulty.

Every psalm is inspired. Over centuries a few have risen as the ones people quote. The series has already touched Psalm 1, 51, 90, 103, and 139. Today finishes with Psalm 34.

🪨 The Mighty Men Began in a Cave

Have you heard of the mighty men of David? Kevin walked through the legends of 2 Samuel 23: Josheb-Basshebeth with a spear against eight hundred; Eleazar fighting until the sword seemed fused to his hand; Shammah holding a field of lentils. Abishai against three hundred. Benaiah killing a lion. Uriah remaining faithful to the end. “These were the mighty men of David — but they weren’t always so mighty.”

David’s rise was messy. After Goliath, Saul grew insecure as David’s star rose. It was not full civil war, but it was enough that David fled — to Gath, a Philistine town, Goliath’s hometown. Recognized, he feigned madness, frothing at the mouth, until he could escape to the Cave of Adullam. That is where the “mighty men” join him. Pay attention to how Scripture describes them:

📖 1 Samuel 22:1–2 (NIV)
“David left Gath and escaped to the cave of Adullam. When his brothers and his father’s household heard about it, they went down to him there. All those who were in distress or in debt or discontented gathered around him, and he became their commander. About four hundred men were with him.”
The rejects of society following their rejected leader — after he has just acted like a crazy person.

Kevin believes this is the moment Psalm 34 is first sung. We do not always get a superscription. Here we do: “Of David. When he pretended to be insane before Abimelek, who drove him away, and he left.” Something about this song, sung in hiding, is part of how distressed men became formidable.

🔤 An Acrostic With a Lesson in the Middle

Psalm 34 is an acrostic: each line walks the Hebrew alphabet. Twenty-two letters, read right to left. Unusually, the sixth letter (vav) is dropped and the seventeenth (pe) is repeated at the end — still twenty-two letters, but the alphabet is altered.

Why? Kevin pointed to a widely held theory (David does something similar in Psalm 25): take the first, middle, and final letters of this altered alphabet — aleph, lamed, pe — and they spell a word related to learn. Because it is an acrostic, the psalm does not have to march in tight logical sequence; the main message often sits in the middle. So the sermon stays with three or four middle verses — then the last verse, which breaks every acrostic convention, as if David is saying: pay attention to this line.

The alphabet, altered

Hebrew would put aleph on the right. The figure runs left to right so English verse numbers increase the way a NIV Bible does. The hole is the missing vav; the last cell is the extra pe. You still count twenty-two written lines.

First, middle, last: a word meaning “learn”

Drop vav (before lamed) and add pe at the end, and the first, middle, and final letters of the written psalm are aleph, lamed, and pe. That is a widely held reading of this psalm and of Psalm 25, not a one-off guess.

🌊 Reverence: Dread and Fascination

Fear of the Lord does not only mean being scared of God. It is multifaceted. Kevin quoted Ralph Hawkins: the hushed, trembling, speechless humility of the creature before what is mystery, inexpressible, above all creatures — curious and drawn in, and also very scared. Two polar impulses: dread and fascination. A word that holds both is reverence.

He has never been scuba diving, but he pictured looking down where the ocean has no visible floor: fear of the unseen water, and wonder at what might be there. That, he said, is the best approximation he has for reverence.

The pastoral problem: reverence stays abstract. It does not matriculate into how we live. The mighty men lived differently. They cultivated this fear until it changed them. Psalm 34:12 names the fruit — if you love life and many good days, fear God — then verses 13–14 give three practices. (Those are unpacked in the Three Practices tab.)

✝️ The Line That Breaks the Poem

Verse 21 has already closed the alphabet with ת (tav). Verse 22 starts with פ a second time — the LORD redeems those who take refuge in him. That is the line that will not stay inside the poem’s rules, so Kevin refused to skip it.

📖 Psalm 34:22 (NIV)
“The LORD will rescue his servants; no one who takes refuge in him will be condemned.”

Cultivate the fear of the Lord and dependence on yourself decreases; dependence on God increases. That is near the heart of the gospel: we were powerless in our sin to help ourselves. Unless you believe that, you will not believe with conviction your need for Jesus and the cross. The good news is that if you look on your helplessness and cry for help, God rescues you. And it is not a single moment. The Christian walk is to come daily to the cross: Lord, I need you.

The most faithful people Kevin looks up to do not simply have a low view of themselves. Their view of God is getting greater. That, he suggested, is why the mighty men could face a horde of Philistines, one against many, even when the army fled — because from that day in the cave they cultivated a fear of God until they could stand before a sea of enemies and not flinch, knowing their God was greater.

🪨 Three Ways to Cultivate the Fear of the Lord

Psalm 34:13–14 is the training ground. Not three slogans — three habits that move reverence from the head into a life.

Practice 01

Restrain and redirect the tongue

Psalm 34:13 — keep the tongue from evil and lips from deceit. Then verse 1: praise continually. Specific, not performative.

Practice 02

Turn from evil; do good as justice

Psalm 34:14a. Isaiah 1:17: seek justice, correct oppression, plead the widow’s cause. Small acts still matter.

Practice 03

Seek shalom and pursue it

Psalm 34:14b. Not mere absence of violence — flourishing as things were designed to be, including body and work.

🗣️ 1. The Tongue: Fire, then Praise

Don’t lie. Don’t curse. Don’t slander. Don’t gossip. Kevin was frank: modern Christianity has downplayed this. Gossip is loved; impulsive cursing is excused as not wanting to be “goody two-shoes.” Scripture is consistent — Old and New — that unrestrained speech has severe consequences. The tongue is a fire (James 3:6). Jesus locates defilement in what comes out of the mouth, and says we will give account for our words. The first telltale of hidden wickedness, even in religious people, is speech. The wise restrain the tongue.

He would not leave it at “stop talking.” The other consistent teaching is to redirect the tongue toward its highest purpose: praise. Psalm 34:1 does not wait for verse 13. “I will extol the LORD at all times; his praise will always be on my lips.”

What is the difference between an athlete’s genuine thanks and performative God-talk after a win? Whether the person can actually track God’s work in a circumstance. Kevin’s tip: be specific.

He had just returned from a Niseko mission trip — 24 people, an English camp. Each night, ministry workers spoke. The first night, the Hong Kong owner of the lodge told how her family, skiing and golfing in Niseko, met Christians with no place to gather. They bought a small property for fellowship. It ballooned into a retreat centre for ministry workers in Japan, for a time housing a church, and drawing other work — including the Christian school the team served. She did not say “God did everything” and sit down. She spent two hours on the finances: improbable funding, Japan’s downturn stretching every dollar, God pausing or expediting so the exact amount met a particular piece of land. The room, mostly from Hong Kong, stayed riveted. Some teared up over balance sheets. That is specific praise.

Not disingenuous “God is good all the time” as a slogan only — say that, and also name the particular mercy you can actually see.

⚖️ 2. Do Good: Justice, Not Mere Kindness

“Turn away from evil and do good” sounds obvious. Scripturally, doing good is more than being a kind person. Isaiah 1:17: learn to do right, seek justice, defend the oppressed, take up the fatherless, plead the widow’s cause. Stand in the gap for people who cannot help themselves.

A few weeks earlier Kevin’s wife spoke at an anti-trafficking conference in Chiang Mai. Two things struck him. First, the amount of evil — including against children as young as six months — is appalling. Second, the people and organizations taking the fight to that enterprise were largely Christian, even though the conference was not explicitly Christian.

He named the pushback: trafficking is too big; poverty is too big; what can I even do? The problems are more complicated than we know, and they feed one another. That is not an excuse to sit on our hands. Two truths: no single person, organization, or movement ends these institutions — it takes everybody stepping up. And every small thing still matters. Safeguarding an elderly person from a scam by teaching them not to share passwords matters. James 1:27: pure religion meets orphans and widows in their affliction.

These problems are bigger than we know. We are more powerful than we know. Do justice even when you do not see the fruit.

🌿 3. Shalom: Things as They Were Designed to Be

“Seek peace and pursue it.” The word is shalom. You cannot define it as the mere absence of violence — this psalm is being heard by four hundred men, many of whom will become warriors. Better: flourishing across God’s creation.

  • Nature: plants growing and fruiting as they should; ecosystems healthy and supporting one another.
  • Human relationships: more than surface; deep trust; outdoing one another in service; dignity for each person as the image of God.
  • God: not haphazard or legalistic duty, but love that wants to learn more of him, dynamic rather than stagnant.

In short: pursue things as they were designed to be. Kevin then refused to leave “spiritual life” sealed off from two ordinary zones.

Physical health

Most animals, he said, were designed for one primary movement — birds to fly, fish to swim. Humans were designed for a vast array. He quoted a Harvard professor (transcript: “John Rady”) calling humans the Swiss Army knives of motion: born to run, yes, and also for much else. We can swim (not like a dolphin) and run (not like a leopard). So go work out. Tell your crunches: I am living after my design. Be in motion, because in doing so you pursue what God intended bodies to be.

Work

Work existed before the fall, therefore work is not a necessary evil and will exist in the renewed world. Sin made work hard; it did not invent work. He cited Al Wolters: the earth had been unformed and empty; in six days God formed it and filled it — but not completely. People must carry on development as God’s representatives. We create, cultivate, and build cities and infrastructures as part of that creative plan. So: accountant, work diligently. Teacher, cultivate a desire for knowledge in children. Artist, paint passionately. Work is continuation of God’s creative process, not a curse to endure until heaven.

🎯 Application

Kevin’s summary: if you want to cultivate the fear of the Lord — restrain the tongue and redirect it toward authentic, specific praise; pattern your life to do justice even when it feels like none of it matters; pursue shalom in every facet, including physical health, work, and relationships. When you do, fascination and dread both increase. God looks larger.

For those who feel the horde: Kevin prayed for people facing what seems insurmountable, trapped in a decision, or scared of consequences in the next few days. The cave does not make God smaller. Cultivated reverence is how the heart catches up with what the head already recites: he is greater.

💭 Reflection & Discussion

📝 From the service

  • Where is your “Cave of Adullam” right now — distress, debt, bitterness of soul — and what would it mean to learn the fear of the Lord there, not after you get out?
  • When you say you “fear God,” is it fright, abstraction, or reverence (dread and fascination together)?

🔍 Going deeper

  • Which of the three practices is weakest in you this month: tongue, justice, or shalom — and what is one honest example?
  • Can you praise God specifically about a circumstance, or only in generalities? What would a “two-hour balance sheet” of gratitude look like for your life?
  • Where have you used “the problem is too big” as a reason to sit on your hands?
  • Do you treat your body and your work as spiritual, or as compartments God does not care about?
  • The people Kevin admires did not mainly shrink themselves; they grew their view of God. What would it look like for your view of God to get larger this week?

🙏 Prayer prompts

  • Tongue: Lord, guard what I say. Teach me specific praise, not empty slogans.
  • Justice: Make us a people who do good — who stand in the gap for those who cannot help themselves.
  • Shalom: Show us how things were meant to be, in our bodies, work, families, and worship, and give us courage to pursue that.
  • Refuge: We know in our heads that you are greater than the horde. Help us believe it in our hearts. Lord, we need you.
